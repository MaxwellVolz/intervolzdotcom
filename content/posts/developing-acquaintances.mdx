Acquaintances is a contacts app organised around how you know people. The whole
product is one screen: a bubble graph of everyone you know, and a row of chips
generated from your own data. Tap a venue and the people from it converge to the
centre while everyone else drifts out and dims.

Live at: [acquaintances.app](https://acquaintances.app)

You rarely forget the context of a person. "The guy I met at Bings who does sound
design" is right there. It is the name that is gone, and every contacts app indexes
on the name.

The interesting part is not the graph. On day one I wrote an architecture decision
record whose last constraint had nothing to do with contacts: **this becomes a
native iOS app later.** Every other decision bent around that sentence for five
days. On day six I collected, and a Swift transliteration of the core came out
bit-identical to the TypeScript.

Here is what that bet cost, and what it paid.

---

## The constraint that shaped everything (Aug 5)

ADR 0001 opens with five hard constraints from the brief:

- 390×844 baseline, one-handed, safe-area aware
- 60fps layout animation at ~300 nodes on a mid-range phone
- every field optional except name
- no accounts, no backend, all data on device
- **it becomes a native iOS app later**

And then the line the rest of the repo is downstream of:

> The last one is the constraint that shapes everything else. A web app that has to
> become a native app is not the same project as a web app.

The obvious move was Expo with `react-native-web` from day one: one codebase, a real
iOS build, no port to plan for. I rejected it. The graph visualisation _is_ the
product, and it needs an imperative `<canvas>`, a custom hit-test, pinch and pan
gestures, and a 60fps render loop. Putting `react-native-web` between the app and the
canvas taxes exactly the part that carries all the risk, and slows the iteration loop
on the part that most needs iterating.

The accepted cost is written into the ADR: `ui/` gets thrown away at port time. The
mitigation is to make `ui/` as thin as possible and put everything that matters
somewhere it survives.

```
ui/  ──────►  core/  ◄──────  platform/
(React,        (pure TS,       (IndexedDB, photos,
 canvas,        no DOM)         haptics, importers)
 gestures)
```

| Layer       | Holds                                                                                    | At port time               |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| `core/`     | data model, layout engine, facet ranking, filtering, validation, the storage _interface_ | moves untouched            |
| `platform/` | IndexedDB, photo downscaling, haptics, clock, id generation, importers                   | one new file per interface |
| `ui/`       | React, canvas, gestures. Owns no rules.                                                  | rewritten                  |

The dependency arrows only ever point one way, and that is enforced by lint rather
than by intent. `eslint.config.js` bans `window`, `document`, `indexedDB`,
`navigator`, `fetch` and `localStorage` inside `core/`, and bans `core/` from
importing `ui/`, `platform/`, React, or any backend SDK. The ADR is blunt about why:

> a boundary maintained by good intentions is a boundary that is gone in six months.

The practical shape of it is that `core/` never calls `Date.now()` or
`crypto.randomUUID()`. It takes a `Clock` and an `IdGenerator` as arguments. That
looks like ceremony for about five days, and then it does not:

> The port requirement and the testability requirement turn out to be the same
> requirement.

## One day to a working graph (Aug 5)

24 commits. The first one in the repository is not the data model and not the app.
It is `Ignore private data before anything else lands`. This is a database of third
parties who never consented to being in it, and the gitignore went in before there
was anything to ignore.

A person has exactly one required field: `name`. Everything else — where you met,
where they live, what they do — is an `{ personId, typeId, value }` row.

![](/imgs/acq_profile.png "The profile sheet. Every row below the name is an attribute, and 'Met at' appearing three times is three rows, not a schema problem.")

That is load-bearing rather than decorative. The filter chips are generated from the
frequency of attribute values across your own graph, so adding a new kind of field
has to be possible without a migration or a UI rewrite. Adding one is a single entry
in `ATTRIBUTE_TYPES`; the add form, the profile sheet, the chip ranker and the
proximity model all read from that list. Star sign is derived from birthday on read
and never stored, so correcting the birthday corrects the sign.

Chips are ranked, not configured. `rankFacets` scores every candidate value by how
many people share it, with diminishing returns so one huge value cannot crowd out
every venue, plus how recently you entered it and how much that _kind_ of attribute
means. Values held by only one person are dropped, because tapping them is just
opening that person the long way round.

![](/imgs/acq_resting.png 'Resting. Everyone weakly held near the origin.')

![](/imgs/acq_filtered.png "Monk's Café, tapped. Twelve of fifty converge; the rest are banished to a far ring and dimmed. The chip row has re-ranked against the slice — the second chip is now 129 West 81st, which was fourth a moment ago.")

Once a filter is on, the remaining chips re-rank against the current slice, so the
row narrows with you. Shared attributes bend the layout but are never materialised as
edges: the edge set stays exactly what you asserted, because turning shared values
into stored relationships would claim friendships that do not exist. Rare shared
values count for far more than common ones. Sharing a six-person bar is signal.
Sharing a city that 194 of your 282 people live in is not, and is ignored outright.

One force drives all three views.

| View     | Target ring                                                                           |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Resting  | everyone weakly held near the origin                                                  |
| Filtered | matches at radius 0, everyone else banished to a far ring and dimmed                  |
| Focused  | the person pinned at the origin, everyone else at a radius set by connection strength |

![](/imgs/acq_focused.png 'Focused on Jerry. George, Kramer and Elaine are nearest because explicit connections outrank inferred ones.')

The simulation is hand-written over `Float32Array`s rather than reaching for
`d3-force`, partly for determinism — d3 jitters coincident nodes with `Math.random`,
which `core/` is not allowed to call — and partly because a force model with no
dependencies is a force model that ports. At 300 nodes a tick costs **0.22ms against
a 6ms budget**, and the render loop parks itself when the simulation settles, so a
graph at rest costs no battery.

## Accounts, without giving up local-first (Aug 6)

28 commits, the heaviest day in the repo. ADR 0001 had said no accounts, so adding
them started by writing down what that gives up rather than quietly amending it.

The shape is the same trick as everywhere else. `core/auth/session.ts` declares an
`AuthPort` and owns nothing that implements it. `platform/supabase/auth.ts` is the
browser's answer, and `LocalOnlyAuth` is the answer when no backend is configured —
which is why nothing outside `boot()` has to branch on whether accounts exist at all.
Leave the environment file absent and the app runs exactly as it always did: signed
out, on device, no network. That is what the public deploy does.

Sync went in the same evening: one comparable stamp on every record, tombstones and a
dirty set, and the merge written as a pure function with no network in it. Row-level
security was proven with two real sessions rather than asserted, and one rule carries
most of the weight — a pulled record is never marked for push, because two devices
bouncing one row between them is the failure that does not stop on its own.

The genuinely hard question was what happens to the graph already on the device.

> `seedSource` says what put the data there, `userAuthored` says whether the user has
> since made it theirs, and both are needed: an untouched demo graph is worth nothing,
> an edited one is worth everything, and the private seed is real people either way.

Upload is a recommendation, never an action. The engine asks through a callback and
does nothing on a no, and deliberately leaves `lastSyncedUserId` unset so the next
sign-in asks afresh instead of remembering a refusal as a decision.

## The bug that ate people (Aug 7)

That design is careful. It shipped broken anyway.

The private seed loads with `seedSource` of `existing` on every boot after the first,
because the source describes _this boot_, not the data's origin. Deciding by source
alone marked exactly the graph the feature exists to protect as discardable. Sign in,
nothing uploads, and `lastSyncedUserId` records the account as synced so it is never
offered again.

Silent and permanent. It was found by its intended owner asking where their people
had gone.

The rule now discards only what is provably synthetic: the demo cast by id, or a
fixture the user never touched. Everything else is somebody's people and gets the
confirm-then-upload path. The lesson is not "test more". It is that `seedSource`
answered a question one word away from the question being asked, and a field that
answers _almost_ the right question reads as correct in every review.

## Going live at acquaintances.app

The deploy is where the privacy posture gets tested, and both ways to get it wrong
are silent.

A plain `vercel deploy` filters uploads through `.gitignore`, and `public/data/` is
gitignored — so it ships the synthetic fixture while looking like a success. The only
sign is `guard: no private data staged` in the build log, which reads like a pass.
And a preview deploy is only safe at all because Vercel Authentication gates it,
which is a property nobody checks after the first time.

So neither is left to discipline. `npm run preview:real` stages the seed, builds
locally, deploys `--prebuilt`, asserts the seed is actually in the output before
uploading, verifies that an anonymous request gets bounced, and deletes
`.vercel/output` afterwards. That last step matters more than it looks: a
`--prebuilt` deploy skips the build entirely, so a stale output directory holding real
data would be published by a `--prod` flag without anything running to stop it.

| Where          | What it serves                                              |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| `npm run dev`  | `data/seed.json` if present, else the demo fixture          |
| preview deploy | whatever was staged at build time, behind auth, `noindex`   |
| production     | the synthetic fixture, always — the build refuses otherwise |

Underneath it, `scripts/guard-private.mjs` runs first in `npm run build` and fails
outright when `public/data/` exists and `VERCEL_ENV` is production. The unsafe
command differs from the safe one by a single flag, so it is blocked rather than
discouraged.

That leaves the public site needing a graph that means something while being nobody
real. Signed-out visitors pick a cast.

![](/imgs/acq_cast.png "Four fixtures, built by scripts in the repo rather than typed in. The 119th Congress is the one that is not a sitcom: real people, public affiliations, and nobody's private notebook.")

## Collecting the bet (Aug 10)

ADR 0001 left one fork open: `core/` moves either as TypeScript under React Native,
or transliterated to Swift. Swift. A JS bridge on the 60fps render path is the one
cost this project has consistently refused to pay everywhere else, and the force
simulation is arithmetic over flat arrays, which is what Swift is good at.

Transliterating 5,472 lines is mechanical. What actually needed deciding is that
three JavaScript semantics the code silently rests on have no Swift equivalent, and
every one of them is invisible until two runs are compared.

| JavaScript leans on                                         | Swift does not have it                                                                                                      | Decision                                                          |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `localeCompare` as the final tie-break in four ranked lists | ICU collation disagrees with Swift's string ordering **in pure ASCII** — ICU puts `apple` before `Apple`, Swift the reverse | replace it on both sides with a comparison over UTF-16 code units |
| `Array.prototype.sort` is stable by spec                    | Swift's `sort()` is an introsort and promises nothing                                                                       | a stable merge sort in `Support/`, used by every core sort        |
| `Map` and `Set` iterate in insertion order                  | Swift's are unordered and hash-seeded per process, so not stable run to run                                                 | hand-rolled `OrderedDictionary` and `OrderedSet`                  |

> Left alone, the three compound: unordered iteration decides ties that an unstable
> sort then scrambles differently on every launch.

Replacing `localeCompare` changes shipped web behaviour — ties between mixed-case
names reorder — so it went in as a deliberate change rather than a refactor that
happens to be safe.

Then the float rule, which is the one that makes the whole thing possible. In
JavaScript a `Float32Array` is storage, not arithmetic: every read widens to float64,
intermediates stay float64, and rounding happens only on store. So Swift holds
`[Float]` for storage and `Double` for every intermediate. Writing the accumulators as
`Float` would round 300 times per node instead of once — and would look like a
faithful transliteration.

**Phase 1 shipped no app.** It ends with `swift test` green and nothing to look at.
That ordering was deliberate: the simulation is the only part of the port that can
fail in a way nobody notices for months, so it went first. Parity vectors record
float32 **bit patterns in hex**, never decimal, and the comparison has no tolerance
anywhere. That is not fastidiousness — reverting one line of the port produces a
1-ULP miss on tick one, `-60.27404` against `-60.274036`, which any tolerance would
have accepted, and which has compounded into visible drift by tick 50.

### What broke

**V8's libm is not Apple's.** Rank scores diverged by 1 to 2 ULP, and the cause was
not the port. Over 80 sampled inputs each, `log1p` disagrees on 4 and `pow` on 11 —
`log1p(2)` among them, so it is not an exotic-input problem. The rule got refined
rather than quietly loosened: values that pass through a float32 store compare
exactly, because the store absorbs the noise and because they feed back into
themselves every tick; sort keys compare rounded to float32 with the resulting
_order_ asserted exactly, because pinning their last bit would pin V8's libm rather
than this app's behaviour.

**Two modules I said were ported were not.** I reported the Phase 1 module list
complete. `graph/candidates.ts` and `model/parse.ts` had no Swift at all. Both suites
were green the whole time, because a module nobody ported is also a module nobody
wrote a test for, and the vector emitter only records functions it is told about. It
was found by diffing `core/` against the Swift sources by hand. The durable fix is
`scripts/check-port-coverage.mjs` in CI: enumerate `src/core/`, demand a Swift
counterpart for each, and make every exception state its reason in writing.

**Swift is the slower one.** ADR 0001 called the simulation "the piece most obviously
suited to Swift, where `Float32Array` becomes `[Float]` and gets faster". It does
not. Release Swift runs ~0.6ms per tick against V8's ~0.2ms, about three times
slower. V8's JIT handles this loop very well, and the port pays for its parity
discipline by converting every stored `Float` to `Double` and back on each access.
The trade is still right — bit-exactness for 3× the time, affordable because 0.6ms
leaves ten times the budget at 300 nodes — but it is the opposite of what the ADR
predicted, and it is now written into the test suite where the next person will find
it. A debug build costs 6.5ms against that same 6ms budget, so the perf suite skips
itself unless built with optimisation, and CI runs it in release.

**An xcconfig ate the backend URL.** Yesterday, wiring Supabase for real: `//` starts
a comment in an xcconfig, so the project URL was silently truncated to `https:`. It
builds, it launches, and every request goes to a nonsense host. Escaped, documented,
and `npm run ios:check` now fails on it.

### Put the graph on a phone

![](/imgs/acq_ios.png 'The native app, first run: SQLite opened, 120 people seeded, the same clustering. The chip row reads Bings 27, Dolores Park 9 because rankFacets is the same function the web app calls.')

Almost none of that app target is new logic. It boots, opens SQLite, loads through
validation, seeds the demo and renders — and every rule it applies lives in
`AcquaintanceCore`, which is what the port was for. `core/` changed by exactly nothing
to gain a real database; a `Repository` opened on SQLite never learns it is not the
in-memory one.

`ui/` was the layer ADR 0001 expected to throw away, and most of it was. The camera
is the exception, ported rather than rewritten: it is pure arithmetic with no DOM in
it, it decides whether a tap lands on the bubble under your finger, and rewriting it
would have meant rediscovering the inset handling by feel.

## Where it is now

Seven days old. 352 tests on the web side, 153 in 30 suites on the Swift side, and 32
of 34 core modules with Swift counterparts — the two that do not are the zod schema
and a test-only fixture file, both declared with their reasons. CI regenerates the
parity vectors on every run and fails if they differ from what was committed, so
changing `core/` without re-emitting them is a failed job rather than a slow surprise.

It still does not work offline. Your people live in IndexedDB on the device, but
there is no service worker caching the app shell, so with no network the page cannot
load at all — the data is there and unreachable. Installing to the home screen gets
you standalone display, not offline. The README said otherwise for about a day before
that got corrected.

## What I learned

**A boundary maintained by good intentions is already gone.** The layer rule is an
ESLint config, not a convention, and it has failed the build several times over seven
days. Every one of those would otherwise have been a small reasonable exception that
made the port a rewrite.

**Determinism is the port.** Passing a `Clock` and an `IdGenerator` into `core/`
looked like ceremony for five days. It is the only reason a Swift transliteration
could be held to the TypeScript bit for bit, and the only reason every test is
reproducible. Those turned out to be the same property, bought once.

**Parity has to be something CI enforces, not something a README claims.** I reported
the module list complete and it was not, with both test suites green. Tests only
cover what somebody remembered to write; the structural check covers what nobody
remembered. If a claim matters, find the check that fails when it stops being true.

**The dangerous deploy is the one that looks like it worked.** Both privacy failures
here print a success line. That is the specific shape worth hunting for — not the
command that errors, but the command that quietly does the wrong thing and logs a
pass.

**Write down what you assumed, so being wrong is cheap.** ADR 0001 predicted Swift
would be faster. It is three times slower. Because the prediction was written down in
a specific, checkable form, measuring it took one afternoon and cost nothing but a
corrected assumption. Vague optimism cannot be falsified, which is what makes it
expensive.
